# Color

## Principle and Context

- Separate hue, lightness, and saturation. Identify whether the problem concerns recognition, hierarchy, mood, readability, or state meaning before choosing a palette.
- Complementary hues can create separation and energy; analogous hues can support continuity. Neither relationship guarantees text contrast, balance, or suitability.
- Judge colors together. Surrounding colors, surface brightness, transparency, and area change their perceived weight and appearance.
- Compare large background fields with small accents at actual size. A color that works in a swatch can dominate a page when its area grows.

## Roles and Repetition

Assign roles to background, surface, text, border, action, selection, status, and brand colors. Reuse a role consistently within its context. Distinguish different meanings even when they share a brand palette.

- Preserve success, warning, error, information, selected, and disabled meanings across relevant screens.
- Use accents in proportion to action and information priority. Multiple accents need distinguishable jobs.
- Keep series, thresholds, legends, selections, and risk ranges stable in data views. Add labels, position, shape, or pattern so color is not the sole carrier of meaning.
- Keep brand recognition separate from action and risk signals. A brand hue is not automatically a suitable error, selection, or CTA color.

## Expression

Choose warm, cool, vivid, muted, pastel, or neutral relationships from the audience, message, environment, and desired impression. Treat them as options, not universal emotional formulas. Consider cultural and domain conventions.

Use solids, gradients, transparency, and patterns to support form, tone, emphasis, or data meaning. Emotional appeal and memorable identity are valid goals when they do not undermine reading or functional meaning.

Maintain a coherent product mood through empty, success, warning, and error states while preserving the distinctions users need.

## Verification

- Inspect actual foreground/background combinations, including transparency, imagery, overlays, hover, focus, and selected states.
- Measure contrast under the applicable accessibility standard before claiming compliance. Name the standard, relevant requirement, and measured evidence; hue difference or visual inspection alone is insufficient.
- Check text, icons, boundaries needed for operation, focus indicators, and state markers separately.
- Verify meaning and hierarchy in the relevant light, dark, high-contrast, and user themes. Check whether grayscale or loss of a hue still leaves an understandable signal.
- Record project-specific roles through existing tokens or documented choices. Do not fabricate a universal numeric palette.
